\section{Purpose}

This document summarizes the state of the art in operational ETAS
(Epidemic-Type Aftershock Sequence) forecasting with a specific focus on
one practical question for this repository:

\begin{quote}
How should we design and evaluate ETAS experiments that compare forecasts
issued from short data windows (hours to days after a mainshock) against
later forecasts for a large New Zealand earthquake such as the
2016 M7.8 Kaik\={o}ura sequence?
\end{quote}

The emphasis is operational rather than purely methodological. The review
therefore concentrates on:

\begin{itemize}
    \item forecast issue times and learning-window lengths used in practice;
    \item short-term aftershock incompleteness and its effect on ETAS fitting;
    \item experiment designs used to evaluate ETAS skill as a function of
          available data;
    \item the role of Reasenberg--Jones (RJ) as the early-forecast baseline;
    \item Kaik\={o}ura-specific ETAS experience;
    \item pyCSEP best practice for comparing forecasts with different time
          windows.
\end{itemize}

\section{Executive Summary}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A fixed 7-day fitting window is not representative of modern
          operational aftershock forecasting. USGS OAF starts within
          tens of minutes and updates repeatedly through the first hours,
          then daily thereafter \cite{usgs_schedule,usgs_overview}.

    \item The dominant technical problem in very early ETAS forecasting is
          short-term aftershock incompleteness (STAI), not lack of model
          sophistication. In the first hours to days after a large mainshock,
          the effective completeness magnitude is elevated and decays with
          time \cite{omi2013,omi2014,page2016,seif2017,lippiello2019,hainzl2024}.

    \item Literature-based ETAS experiments usually vary the issue time
          while keeping the target forecast horizon fixed. That is the clean
          way to isolate the value of additional data
          \cite{omi2013,omi2015,harte2019}.

    \item RJ remains the correct operational baseline for the earliest issue
          times because it is robust with little sequence data. ETAS becomes
          more attractive once aftershocks accumulate or incompleteness is
          handled explicitly \cite{reasenberg1989,usgs_overview,puertorico2022}.

    \item The Kaik\={o}ura case is a warning against naive early ETAS fitting.
          Harte's real-time forecast evaluation found that ETAS
          underforecasted immediately after the mainshock and overforecasted
          later, in part because the earliest catalog was incomplete and the
          mainshock magnitude itself was revised substantially
          \cite{harte2019}.

    \item For pyCSEP, forecasts with different start/end times are distinct
          forecasts and should be evaluated separately with matched observed
          catalogs. Catalog-based tests are the appropriate primary framework
          for ETAS synthetic catalogs \cite{pycsep_eval,pycsep_catalog_tutorial}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Operational Practice: How Early Are Aftershock Forecasts Issued?}

\subsection{USGS Operational Aftershock Forecasting (OAF)}

USGS operational practice is the clearest evidence that early forecasting is
not built around a 7-day waiting period. The official automatic update schedule
states that forecasts are initiated at 10, 30, and 50 minutes after a
mainshock, then at 2, 4, 6, 12, and 18 hours, and then daily after that
\cite{usgs_schedule}. The overview and background material also note that the
current OAF system supports both Reasenberg--Jones and ETAS-based forecasts,
using generic, sequence-specific, and Bayesian parameterizations depending on
how much information is available \cite{usgs_overview,usgs_background}.

Operational implication:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the earliest useful forecast is issued with very little sequence data;
    \item later updates are expected to improve as the catalog stabilizes;
    \item forecast issuance is continuous, not ``fit once at 7 days''.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{CSEP Short-Term ETAS Experiments}

In prospective CSEP-style testing, ETAS is typically evaluated through rolling
short-horizon forecasts rather than through one delayed retrospective fit.
Zhuang's Japan study produced next-day ETAS forecasts for the whole region
\cite{zhuang2011}. The California ETAS archive distributed through CSEP is also
organized as a daily short-term forecast product over 2007--2018
\cite{csep_etas}.

Operational implication:
\begin{itemize}
    \item CSEP standardizes the forecast window and archive process;
    \item the issue-time ladder is often daily, but the same logic applies to
          sub-daily windows if the catalog quality supports them.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{GNS Science / GeoNet New Zealand}

The Kaik\={o}ura operational experience did not revolve around a single ETAS
forecast issued one week after the event. Rhoades et al.\ report that public
forecasts were issued immediately after the earthquake, with 1-day, 7-day, and
30-day horizons, initially using Omori-law decay and then a STEP model after
two days. ETAS forecasts were run in parallel as a real-time experiment
\cite{rhoades2017}.

Operational implication:
\begin{itemize}
    \item New Zealand practice already recognized the need for immediate public
          guidance;
    \item ETAS was treated as part of a broader operational toolkit, not as the
          only early-sequence model.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{INGV / OEF-Italy}

Italy's OEF system is another operational example of repeated issuance rather
than delayed fitting. The establishment paper and later validation work describe
an operational workflow that updates forecasts on a regular schedule and after
significant events, with weekly target forecasts used in Civil Protection
contexts \cite{marzocchi2014,spassiani2023}.

Operational implication:
\begin{itemize}
    \item operational systems frequently choose a fixed forecast horizon
          (for Italy, one week) while updating the model repeatedly;
    \item the natural comparison axis is issue time, not only absolute time
          since the mainshock.
\end{itemize}

\section{Short-Term Catalog Incompleteness and ETAS}

\subsection{Why Incompleteness Dominates the Early Problem}

The first hours after a large mainshock are the hardest interval for ETAS
fitting because the catalog is transiently incomplete. Small events are missed
when seismic phases overlap, codas mask one another, and routine detection and
phase association are stressed by extreme rate bursts. Lippiello et al.\
review this as a central post-seismic forecasting issue rather than a minor
catalog nuisance \cite{lippiello2019}.

This matters because ETAS uses the early aftershock rate and magnitude
distribution to infer productivity, Omori decay, and branching structure.
If many small aftershocks are missing, ETAS can misestimate:

\begin{itemize}
    \item the short-time offset parameter $c$;
    \item the Omori exponent $p$;
    \item the productivity parameters $a$ and $a_{ms}$;
    \item the effective branching ratio;
    \item uncertainty in all of the above.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Key Early-Sequence Papers}

Ogata and Katsura developed one of the early explicit approaches to immediate
aftershock forecasting under incomplete detection by modeling the detection
rate itself \cite{ogata2006}. Omi et al.\ then pushed this operationally:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Omi et al.\ (2013)} tested forecasts based on the first 3, 6,
          12, and 24 hours after a mainshock and showed that incompleteness
          correction materially improved forecasting performance
          \cite{omi2013}.
    \item \textbf{Omi et al.\ (2014)} showed that ETAS parameters can be
          estimated from an early incomplete sequence when the missing-data
          process is modeled explicitly \cite{omi2014}.
    \item \textbf{Omi et al.\ (2015)} found that Bayesian and ensemble ETAS
          forecasts are more stable than single plug-in forecasts when only
          early, uncertain data are available \cite{omi2015}.
\end{itemize}

The operational USGS synthesis by Page et al.\ identifies
time-dependent catalog incompleteness as one of the three essential ingredients
for improved global aftershock forecasts \cite{page2016}. Seif et al.\
quantified the statistical damage from ignoring missing data and truncation:
ETAS parameter estimates shift substantially, and uncertainty can be
dramatically underestimated \cite{seif2017}.

\subsection{How Large Is the Effect?}

For large earthquakes the early loss of completeness can last longer than many
practitioners assume. Hainzl et al.\ report for the 2023 SE T\"urkiye sequence
that, after an M7.7 mainshock, M4 events were not fully recorded during the
first two hours and M3 events remained incomplete for roughly two days
\cite{hainzl2024}. van der Elst's \emph{b-positive} work independently shows
that even basic magnitude-frequency estimation is biased under transient
incompleteness unless the estimator is adapted \cite{vandere1st2021}.
Woessner and Wiemer remain the standard reference for estimating magnitude of
completeness and its uncertainty, which is directly relevant when designing
threshold or sensitivity studies for early-window ETAS experiments
\cite{woessner2005}.

Inference for New Zealand:
\begin{itemize}
    \item if Kaik\={o}ura-like experiments use a fixed $M_c = 3.0$ starting at
          2 hours after origin time, the catalog is unlikely to be complete;
    \item the earliest windows should therefore be treated as either
          incompleteness-aware ETAS fits or higher-threshold fits;
    \item otherwise, apparent differences between ``2 h ETAS'' and ``7 d ETAS''
          may be driven mainly by changing detectability rather than by the true
          value of extra sequence information.
\end{itemize}

\section{Reasenberg--Jones Versus ETAS in Early Forecasting}

The operational distinction between RJ and ETAS is fundamental.

\subsection{Reasenberg--Jones}

The classical Reasenberg--Jones model expresses aftershock occurrence through
mainshock-driven Omori decay plus Gutenberg--Richter magnitude scaling
\cite{reasenberg1989}. In practice this gives a stable, quickly deployable
forecast, especially when no reliable short-term sequence catalog yet exists.
This simplicity is precisely why RJ remains operationally useful.

\subsection{ETAS}

ETAS generalizes the process by allowing every earthquake to generate offspring,
including higher-order triggering and, in many implementations, explicit spatial
kernels \cite{ogata1988}. ETAS is therefore better suited to complex cascading
sequences, migration, and multi-fault ruptures. The trade-off is that ETAS is
more sensitive to early missing data, magnitude revisions, and region/model
misspecification.

\subsection{Operational Lesson}

USGS documentation still presents RJ and ETAS as complementary operational
tools, not as a simple ``old model versus new model'' replacement
\cite{usgs_overview}. The Puerto Rico prospective evaluation is particularly
important: van der Elst et al.\ found that the ETAS-based operational forecast
substantially improved on the existing public RJ forecast for the
2019--2021 sequence \cite{puertorico2022}. The lesson is not that RJ is
obsolete, but that:

\begin{itemize}
    \item RJ is the correct baseline for the earliest issue times;
    \item ETAS should be expected to outperform once the catalog quality and
          sequence information are sufficient, especially for complex sequences.
\end{itemize}

This interpretation is also consistent with the broader operational review of
aftershock forecasting by Hardebeck et al., which emphasizes both the enduring
value of simple statistical baselines and the growing importance of more
flexible triggering models in operational settings \cite{hardebeck2024}.

\section{How the Literature Evaluates Data-Window Length}

\subsection{The Dominant Experimental Pattern}

The strongest experiment designs in the literature vary the issue time while
keeping the target forecast horizon fixed.

Examples:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Omi et al.\ (2013): compare 3 h, 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h learning windows
          for later aftershock forecasting \cite{omi2013}.
    \item Omi et al.\ (2015): fit from the first day and forecast the next
          month using Bayesian and ensemble approaches \cite{omi2015}.
    \item Harte (2019): evaluate forecasts archived at their actual issue
          times during Kaik\={o}ura \cite{harte2019}.
    \item OEF-Italy: repeatedly issue weekly forecasts from a rolling system
          rather than from a single delayed fit \cite{spassiani2023}.
\end{itemize}

This design isolates the information value of an additional 4 hours, 18 hours,
or 6 days of data. It avoids a common confounding mistake: changing both the
issue time and the verification target at the same time.

\subsection{Recommended Design Principle}

For a clean early-versus-late ETAS comparison, define the experiment around
\emph{issue times} and \emph{fixed forecast targets}.

Recommended issue times for this repository:
\[
    2~\text{h},\ 6~\text{h},\ 12~\text{h},\ 1~\text{d},\ 2~\text{d},\ 3~\text{d},\ 7~\text{d}
\]

Recommended forecast targets for each issue time:
\[
    \text{next 1 day},\ \text{next 7 days},\ \text{next 30 days}
\]

This yields a matrix in which:
\begin{itemize}
    \item rows represent how much data were available at issuance;
    \item columns represent the operational decision horizon;
    \item each cell is a distinct forecast that must be evaluated against a
          matching observed subcatalog.
\end{itemize}

\section{Kaik\={o}ura-Specific Lessons}

Harte's real-time forecast evaluation is the most directly relevant
Kaik\={o}ura ETAS study for experiment design in this repository
\cite{harte2019}. Its central findings are operationally important:

\begin{itemize}
    \item ETAS underforecasted immediately after the mainshock;
    \item ETAS overforecasted later in the sequence;
    \item the initial mainshock magnitude was too small and was revised upward
          substantially over the next days;
    \item the earliest catalog quality was poor enough to affect forecast
          performance materially.
\end{itemize}

Those results strongly support two design decisions for this repo:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item explicitly track which mainshock magnitude version and catalog version
          were available at each issue time;
    \item treat very early ETAS windows as a data-quality experiment, not just
          as a parameter-update experiment.
\end{enumerate}

Rhoades et al.\ provide the complementary operational picture: New Zealand
needed public 1-day, 7-day, and 30-day products immediately, so simpler models
were used operationally while ETAS was evaluated in parallel
\cite{rhoades2017}. That is a realistic benchmark philosophy for the present
project as well.

\section{pyCSEP Best Practice for Different Time Windows}

The pyCSEP documentation is explicit that catalog-based forecasts and
observations must match in time, space, and magnitude domain
\cite{pycsep_eval,pycsep_catalog_tutorial}. For this project the key rules are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item forecasts with different start or end times are conceptually separate
          forecasts;
    \item observed catalogs must be filtered to the exact same
          start/end time, spatial region, and magnitude threshold as the
          forecast being evaluated;
    \item ETAS synthetic catalogs should be evaluated primarily with the
          catalog-based tests:
          \begin{itemize}
              \item \texttt{number\_test},
              \item \texttt{magnitude\_test},
              \item \texttt{spatial\_test},
              \item \texttt{pseudolikelihood\_test},
              \item \texttt{resampled\_magnitude\_test},
              \item \texttt{mll\_magnitude\_test},
              \item \texttt{calibration\_test} when enough forecasts are
                    available.
          \end{itemize}
    \item paired T and W tests are not the primary framework for comparing raw
          stochastic ETAS catalog forecasts; those belong to a gridded
          comparative setting and can become unstable in the presence of
          zero-support bins.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Model Branches}

The literature supports running at least two ETAS branches:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Fixed-threshold ETAS}: use the current repository design with
          constant $M_c$.
    \item \textbf{Incompleteness-aware ETAS}: either model
          $M_c(t)$ explicitly or raise the effective threshold in the earliest
          windows.
\end{enumerate}

Recommended benchmarks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item generic RJ;
    \item Bayesian RJ or sequence-specific RJ if available;
    \item generic ETAS for the earliest issue times;
    \item STEP or a simpler short-term operational alternative where possible.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation Plan}

For each issue time and each target horizon:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item generate the ETAS synthetic catalog forecast;
    \item build the matching observed catalog for the exact
          verification window;
    \item run pyCSEP catalog-based tests;
    \item archive the forecast and results prospectively or
          pseudo-prospectively;
    \item compare skill as a function of issue time while keeping the target
          horizon fixed.
\end{enumerate}

Primary reported metrics should be:
\begin{itemize}
    \item N-test quantiles;
    \item M-test and resampled M-test quantiles;
    \item S-test and PL-test quantiles, with explicit unsupported-event counts;
    \item calibration only when many forecasts of the same design are
          available.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Repository-Specific Implementation Notes}

This repository already contains a useful ladder of issue-time configs:
\texttt{etas\_config\_2h.json},
\texttt{etas\_config\_12h.json},
\texttt{etas\_config\_1d.json},
\texttt{etas\_config\_2d.json},
\texttt{etas\_config\_3d.json}, and
\texttt{etas\_config.json} for the 7-day benchmark.

The current update adds the missing 6-hour configuration so that the experiment
ladder better matches the early-window literature. The next methodological
improvement should be a separate branch with time-dependent completeness or a
systematic early-window magnitude-threshold sensitivity study.

\section{Bottom-Line Recommendations}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Do not treat the 7-day fit as the operational default for early
          forecasting. It is a useful benchmark, not the state of the art.
    \item Compare ETAS across issue times, not only across parameter sets.
    \item Use fixed target horizons (next 1 day, next 7 days, next 30 days).
    \item Expect the first hours to be completeness-limited.
    \item Keep RJ as the baseline at very early issue times.
    \item Evaluate ETAS with pyCSEP catalog-based tests using exactly matched
          observed windows.
    \item For Kaik\={o}ura-like sequences, track mainshock magnitude revision,
          catalog version, and unsupported observed events explicitly.
\end{enumerate}
