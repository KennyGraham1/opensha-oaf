\begin{abstract}
We evaluate issue-time sensitivity of operational New Zealand ETAS forecasts
for the 2016 $M_w$~7.8 Kaik\={o}ura sequence using a fixed-horizon design:
all eight forecasts verify the same days 7--14 target window
($N_{\mathrm{obs}}=323$, $M\geq\Mc=3.0$), while only the fit-data window varies
(generic-parameter run, plus sequence-specific fits at 2~h, 6~h, 12~h, 1~d,
2~d, 3~d, and 7~d). Each run contains 1000 stochastic catalogs and is evaluated
with pyCSEP catalog-based tests against a common GeoNet catalog.

Results separate into three regimes. (1) Generic/2~h--1~d runs underpredict
substantially (medians 45--86; ratios 0.14--0.27) and converge to low-cascade
solutions (indirect triggering 2--9\%). (2) 2~d--3~d runs recover branching
($\sim$31\% indirect triggering) but overpredict counts (medians 492--499;
ratios 1.52--1.55), consistent with shallow fitted Omori decay ($p=0.90$).
(3) The 7~d run is count- and temporally calibrated (median 334; ratio 1.03;
rolling Kolmogorov--Smirnov $p=0.98$). Daily-bin diagnostics show that the
7~d weekly success reflects partial compensation of mixed-sign daily errors.

Magnitude diagnostics (resampled M-test and marginal log-likelihood) pass for
all issue times, indicating stable Gutenberg--Richter behavior decoupled from
count calibration. Spatial support remains incomplete in every run
(16--25 observed events in zero-rate cells). In probabilistic terms, all
non-7~d runs are both biased and under-covered at the 90\% level
(0\% empirical coverage), whereas the 7~d run achieves full 90\% coverage.

Compared with a fixed-parameter Reasenberg--Jones benchmark ($p=1.0$,
$c=0.1$~d), ETAS achieves lower weekly CRPS only at 7~d; earlier windows are
better represented by RJ. An operational decision table therefore yields NO-GO
for all pre-7~d ETAS issue times and GO at 7~d for this sequence. Segmented
tests support a primary transition near 1--2~d and weaker evidence for a second
late-window transition in this single-sequence, eight-point issue-time archive.
\end{abstract}

\textbf{Keywords}: ETAS; operational aftershock forecasting; pyCSEP;
Kaik\={o}ura; catalog incompleteness; New Zealand.

\section{Introduction}

Operational aftershock forecasting is fundamentally an issue-time problem: the
question is not only whether a model is good, but whether it becomes useful
quickly enough after a large mainshock to support decisions in the first hours
to days. Modern operational systems therefore do not wait a week before issuing
aftershock forecasts. USGS OAF begins automatic updates within minutes and then
reissues through the first hours and days (USGS operational pages listed in
Data and Resources). The public OAF framework spans both Reasenberg--Jones and
ETAS-style forecasts \cite{reasenberg1989,puertorico2022}.
Prospective CSEP experiments have similarly emphasized short-horizon,
rolling forecasts rather than a single retrospective fit \cite{zhuang2011,
csep_etas}.
Italy's operational earthquake forecasting program follows the same repeated
issuance logic rather than a single delayed fit
\cite{marzocchi2014,spassiani2023}.

The main obstacle to early ETAS forecasting is not lack of model flexibility but
short-term aftershock incompleteness (STAI). In the first hours to days after a
large earthquake, detection thresholds are elevated and many small events are
temporarily missing from routine catalogs
\cite{ogata2006,omi2013,omi2014,page2016,seif2017,lippiello2019,hainzl2024}.
Magnitude-of-completeness estimation therefore becomes part of the forecast
problem itself \cite{woessner2005}, and even magnitude-frequency estimation can
be biased unless transient incompleteness is handled explicitly
\cite{vandere1st2021}.
That affects ETAS productivity, Omori decay, branching, and uncertainty.
Kaik\={o}ura is a strong test case because it was both highly productive and
operationally important, and because the real-time forecast literature already
documents difficulties with the early sequence \cite{rhoades2017,harte2019}.

The goal of this manuscript is to characterize, using a completed fixed-horizon
experiment set, precisely \emph{how} and \emph{why} ETAS forecast skill changes
as the amount of early post-mainshock data increases from hours to days. The
fixed-horizon design---all eight forecasts verifying the same days 7--14
window---is now implemented and allows clean, controlled comparisons across
issue times. Our analysis shows that issue-time effects do not degrade
monotonically but instead produce three qualitatively distinct regimes, each
with a different mechanistic explanation. Recognizing these regimes clarifies
what improvements are most needed and where the leverage is greatest.

The analysis is organized around four explicit questions:
(1) at what issue time does ETAS transition from severe count bias to
approximately calibrated weekly behavior, (2) whether that transition is
explained by branching-structure recovery, Omori-decay recovery, or both,
(3) whether distribution-level reliability (coverage and proper scores) agrees
with pass/fail consistency tests, and (4) when ETAS first outperforms a robust
Reasenberg--Jones baseline in the same fixed verification window.

\section{Background and Positioning}

\subsection{Operational aftershock forecasting systems}

Operational earthquake forecasting (OEF) systems in the U.S., Italy, and
New Zealand share a common structure: rapid initial issuance followed by
frequent updates as sequence-specific information accumulates.
USGS public aftershock products now operate with ETAS-based workflows and have
been evaluated both prospectively and retrospectively
\cite{puertorico2022,page2016}. In California, UCERF3-ETAS demonstrated how
short-term clustering models can be integrated with long-term fault-based
hazard components in an operational framework \cite{field2017ucerf3etas}.
In Italy, OEF development and long-duration validation emphasize repeated
prospective testing rather than one-off retrospective fits
\cite{marzocchi2014,spassiani2023}. In New Zealand, Kaik\={o}ura-era aftershock
products were used directly in agency decision contexts, with documented
communication and interpretation challenges \cite{becker2020kaikoura}.
Recent cross-system syntheses now provide a broader benchmark for comparing
design choices in operational systems and testing pipelines
\cite{mizrahi2024oefreview,han2025oef,serafini2025benchmark}.

Across these systems, the methodological challenge is consistent:
\emph{issue-time forecasting} must balance speed and stability. Early forecasts
are needed operationally, but parameter identifiability and catalog quality are
least favorable at those times. This motivates explicit issue-time experiments
instead of only retrospective ``best-fit'' analyses.

\subsection{New Zealand-specific scientific and operational background}

The New Zealand setting is structurally distinct from many single-fault
aftershock case studies because it straddles an oblique plate-boundary
transition (Hikurangi subduction to Alpine transform), with strong spatial
heterogeneity in faulting style and coupling. The 2016 Kaik\={o}ura mainshock
itself ruptured a complex multi-fault system over $>$170 km
\cite{hamling2017kaikoura}, which makes issue-time ETAS calibration especially
sensitive to early catalog quality, spatial kernel assumptions, and
parameter-identifiability limits.

New Zealand also has a long operational lineage directly relevant to this
manuscript. The New Zealand CSEP testing center formalized prospective model
testing workflows and consistency tests for the region
\cite{gerstenberger2010nztest}. During the Canterbury sequence, hybrid
time-dependent hazard workflows were deployed and evaluated for decision support
\cite{gerstenberger2016canterbury}. More recently, national-scale model updates
in the 2022 Aotearoa NSHM revision and the post-Kaik\={o}ura
time-dependent hazard studies have emphasized explicit treatment of
time-dependent seismicity, model uncertainty, and reproducible testing
\cite{gerstenberger2024nshm,gerstenberger2024srm,gerstenberger2023kaikourahaz}.
These studies provide the immediate NZ context for interpreting ETAS behavior
across issue times in this work.

\subsection{Short-term incompleteness and ETAS identifiability}

Short-term aftershock incompleteness (STAI) is one of the most persistent
sources of early-forecast bias. The first hours to days after a large event are
typically affected by elevated detection thresholds and waveform overlap, which
preferentially removes smaller events from catalogs. Classical catalog-quality
work emphasized that completeness magnitude is time- and context-dependent
\cite{woessner2005}. Operational aftershock studies then quantified the forecast
impact: Page et al.\ showed that time-dependent completeness correction is a
core ingredient for global operational forecasts \cite{page2016}; Seif et al.\
demonstrated strong parameter distortions from missing data and truncation in
ETAS estimation \cite{seif2017}; Omi et al.\ showed that early ETAS fitting can
be made useful only when incompleteness is modeled explicitly
\cite{omi2013,omi2014,omi2015}. More recent work extends this line by developing
robust summary statistics for transiently incomplete catalogs
\cite{vandere1st2021,hainzl2024}.
For New Zealand specifically, ETAS fitting studies have also shown that
background-model misspecification and boundary effects can induce systematic
parameter bias if not explicitly controlled \cite{harte2013bias}.

The literature therefore establishes a clear baseline: completeness correction
is necessary. What is less well resolved is whether correction alone is enough
to recover stable branching and Omori parameters at very short issue times.
The present fixed-horizon experiment directly addresses that identifiability
question.

\subsection{Reasenberg--Jones versus ETAS in early windows}

Reasenberg--Jones (RJ) and ETAS occupy complementary operational roles.
RJ provides a low-dimensional, robust baseline that can be issued quickly with
limited data \cite{reasenberg1989}. ETAS adds secondary triggering, enabling
more realistic distributional forecasts as data accumulate
\cite{ogata1988,ogata2006}. Empirical operational comparisons generally find
that ETAS provides substantial skill gains once the sequence is sufficiently
resolved; the USGS Puerto Rico evaluation reported clear ETAS improvements over
RJ in retrospective comparison while retaining prospective operational utility
\cite{puertorico2022}.

This motivates a regime-based view rather than a model-wins-always framing:
very early windows may favor robust baselines or strong priors, whereas ETAS
advantages grow as branching and decay parameters become identifiable.
Our design tests that transition directly by holding the verification horizon
fixed while varying only the fit-data window.

\subsection{Prospective CSEP evaluation frameworks and scoring}

CSEP and RELM established the prospective, standardized framework that underpins
modern forecast testing \cite{schorlemmer2007relm,schorlemmer2010relm}.
Likelihood-based consistency tests for count, space, and magnitude were
formalized for common space-rate-magnitude forecast formats
\cite{zechar2010likelihood}. Model-comparison tests were then extended with
computationally efficient paired statistics, including the T-test paradigm
\cite{rhoades2011efficient}. Prospective multi-timescale experiments in Italy
highlighted that model skill can vary strongly with forecast horizon and
forecast class, reinforcing the need for horizon-specific evaluation
\cite{taroni2018italy}. Recent CSEP products now provide larger prospective
benchmark datasets and reproducible software pipelines for deeper forecast
intercomparison \cite{serafini2025benchmark}. pyCSEP inherits these principles and makes them
operationally accessible in reproducible workflows.

This background has two implications for the current manuscript.
First, pass/fail consistency tests remain essential but are not sufficient for
mechanistic interpretation. Second, full-distribution diagnostics (proper
scores, PIT behavior, and distributional distance) are needed to resolve
why forecasts pass or fail across issue times, not only whether they pass.

\subsection{Kaik\={o}ura-specific context and remaining gap}

Kaik\={o}ura has already been studied as a real-time stochastic-forecast testbed
\cite{harte2019}, and operational products issued during the sequence are
well documented \cite{rhoades2017,becker2020kaikoura}. However, most prior
analyses do not isolate issue-time effects from horizon effects with a strict
fixed-verification design. As a result, it has been difficult to separate
true early-parameter identifiability limits from simple differences in
verification period difficulty.

The primary gap addressed here is therefore not ``whether ETAS works'' in a
global sense, but \emph{when and why} ETAS transitions between failure and
calibration regimes for one operationally important sequence under controlled
verification conditions. The fixed-horizon architecture, combined with
distribution-level diagnostics, is the key methodological contribution in that
context.

\section{Experiment Set}

\subsection{Fixed-horizon design}

This manuscript analyzes the completed run set archived in
\texttt{nz\_etas\_simulations*.txt} and the corresponding
\texttt{build/pycsep*} directories generated on 31~March--1~April 2026. Those
outputs define the experiment actually evaluated here and should be treated as
the authoritative record for this manuscript.

The archive contains one generic-parameter ETAS run and seven sequence-specific
ETAS runs at increasing issue times:
\[
0.5~\mathrm{d\ generic\text{-}conditioned},\ 2~\mathrm{h},\ 6~\mathrm{h},\
12~\mathrm{h},\ 1~\mathrm{d},\ 2~\mathrm{d},\ 3~\mathrm{d},\ 7~\mathrm{d}.
\]
The defining feature of the design is that \emph{every} run verifies the same
7-day forecast window (days 7.0--14.0 post-mainshock) against the same GeoNet
observed catalog ($N_{\mathrm{obs}}=323$, $M\geq3.0$, NZ testing region).
Because the verification target never changes, differences in pyCSEP test
outcomes are attributable solely to changes in the fitting data window, not
to differences in the forecast horizon or observation period.

Each run produces 1000 stochastic simulations with $\Mc=3.0$,
$M_{\mathrm{max}}=9.5$, and a maximum of 10 ETAS generations.
The early issue-time branch (2~h, 6~h, 12~h, 1~d) uses
\texttt{timeDependentMc = true} to mitigate catalog incompleteness during
fitting; the 2~d, 3~d, and 7~d runs use \texttt{timeDependentMc = false},
consistent with the expectation that catalog completeness is largely recovered
within two days for this sequence.

\subsection{Design decisions}

Three aspects of the design deserve explicit statement.
First, the \emph{generic} run is not a strict time-zero baseline: it is
initialized with the first 0.5~d aftershock list and then uses NZ generic
prior parameters without sequence-specific MLE fitting. It is therefore a
\emph{generic-parameter conditioned} forecast, which provides a meaningful
no-fit reference but is not strictly equivalent to a pre-mainshock prior.
Second, the \texttt{timeDependentMc} branch choice is a scientifically
motivated design decision that is itself part of the experiment: as shown in
Section~\ref{sec:mag_freq}, it is necessary but not sufficient to resolve early
forecast skill.
Third, any comparison of count results between issue times is clean only under
the fixed-horizon design; the reader should not compare these results to any
earlier version of this experiment that used varying verification windows.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{ETAS mathematical formulation}

We use a standard ETAS point-process formulation \cite{ogata1988}. Let
$\mathcal{H}_t$ denote the event history up to time $t$. The conditional
space--time--magnitude intensity can be written as
\begin{equation}
\lambda(t,\mathbf{x},m \mid \mathcal{H}_t) =
\mu(\mathbf{x}) f_M(m) +
\sum_{i:t_i<t} \kappa_i \, g(t-t_i; p,c)\, s(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_i; m_i)\, f_M(m),
\label{eq:etas_intensity}
\end{equation}
where $\mu(\mathbf{x})$ is background spatial rate, $f_M(m)$ is the magnitude
density, $g(\tau;p,c)$ is the Omori temporal kernel, and $s(\cdot)$ is the
spatial triggering kernel.

Magnitudes follow a truncated Gutenberg--Richter form
\begin{equation}
f_M(m)=\beta \exp\!\left[-\beta (m-\Mc)\right], \qquad
\beta = b \ln 10,\quad m\in[\Mc,M_{\max}],
\label{eq:gr_density}
\end{equation}
with $\Mc=3.0$, $b=1.0$, and $M_{\max}=9.5$.

Productivity is parameterized as
\begin{equation}
\kappa_i=
\begin{cases}
10^{a_{ms}+\alpha(m_i-M_{\mathrm{ref}})}, & \text{mainshock seed},\\[2pt]
10^{a+\alpha(m_i-M_{\mathrm{ref}})},      & \text{other triggering events},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:productivity}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha=1$ and $M_{\mathrm{ref}}=4.5$, and
\begin{equation}
g(\tau;p,c)=K_t(\tau+c)^{-p}, \qquad \tau>0,\; c>0,\; p>0,\; K_t>0,
\label{eq:omori_kernel}
\end{equation}
where $K_t$ is the implementation-consistent temporal scaling constant, treated
as fixed for cross-run diagnostic comparisons.

The expected count in window
$[t_s,t_e]\times R\times[\Mc,M_{\max}]$ is
\begin{equation}
\hat N_{[t_s,t_e]} =
\int_{t_s}^{t_e}\!\!\int_R\!\!\int_{\Mc}^{M_{\max}}
\lambda(t,\mathbf{x},m \mid \mathcal{H}_t)\,dm\,d\mathbf{x}\,dt.
\label{eq:expected_count}
\end{equation}
To track cascade stability in each fitted regime, we use a
\emph{finite-horizon} branching diagnostic (here, $H=7$ days):
\begin{equation}
\bar n_H =
\int_{\Mc}^{M_{\max}}
\kappa(m)\,G_H(p,c)\,f_M(m)\,dm,
\label{eq:branching_ratio}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
G_H(p,c)=\int_0^H g(\tau;p,c)\,d\tau
=
K_t
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{(H+c)^{1-p}-c^{1-p}}{1-p}, & p\neq 1,\\[8pt]
\log\!\left(\dfrac{H+c}{c}\right), & p=1.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:kernel_mass_h}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa(m)=10^{a+\alpha(m-M_{\mathrm{ref}})}$ for non-mainshock
triggering events. This avoids the divergent $[0,\infty)$ integral when
$p\le1$ and keeps the diagnostic well-defined for all fitted runs in this
experiment. Accordingly, $\bar n_H$ is interpreted here as a finite-horizon
cascade-stability indicator (not a claim of an asymptotic Galton--Watson
branching ratio). We also report simulation-based indirect triggering share
\begin{equation}
\phi_{\mathrm{ind}} =
100\times\frac{N_{\mathrm{tot}}-N_{\mathrm{direct}}}{N_{\mathrm{tot}}},
\label{eq:indirect_share}
\end{equation}
with $N_{\mathrm{direct}}$ denoting events in generation 1
(directly triggered by the mainshock seed).

\subsection{Likelihood and parameter estimation}

For each issue time, sequence-specific runs estimate
$\theta=(a_{ms},a,p,c)$ by maximizing a completeness-weighted log-likelihood
over the training window:
\begin{equation}
\ell(\theta)=
\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{train}}}
w_j \log \lambda(t_j,\mathbf{x}_j,m_j \mid \mathcal{H}_{t_j};\theta)
-\int_{T_0}^{T_1}\!\!\int_R\!\!\int_{\Mc}^{M_{\max}}
\lambda(t,\mathbf{x},m \mid \mathcal{H}_t;\theta)\,dm\,d\mathbf{x}\,dt.
\label{eq:weighted_ll}
\end{equation}
When \texttt{timeDependentMc=true}, the weights $w_j$ adjust for transient
completeness; when \texttt{false}, $w_j=1$.

The optimizer is a discrete grid search (from archived configuration):
\begin{equation}
a_{ms}\in[-4,1]\;(21),\quad
a\in[-3.5,-0.5]\;(21),\quad
p\in[0.5,2.0]\;(16),\quad
c\in[10^{-4},10^{-1}]\;(16),
\label{eq:grid_ranges}
\end{equation}
where parentheses denote grid-node counts. This finite grid is relevant for
interpretation: adjacent issue times can return identical parameter vectors if
the likelihood maximum remains on the same node.

\subsection{Stochastic forecast generation}

For each fitted parameter set, 1000 synthetic catalogs are generated via a
branching simulation:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item initialize parent events from fitted history and mainshock seed;
    \item sample offspring counts from Poisson rates implied by
          Equations~\ref{eq:etas_intensity}--\ref{eq:omori_kernel};
    \item sample offspring times from the Omori kernel, magnitudes from
          Equation~\ref{eq:gr_density}, and locations from the spatial kernel;
    \item recurse until generation limit (10) or extinction.
\end{enumerate}
Catalogs are truncated to $M\ge\Mc$ and evaluated in the NZ testing region over
the fixed forecast window (days 7.0--14.0).

\subsection{pyCSEP evaluation}

We evaluate each forecast using pyCSEP catalog-based tests against a GeoNet
observed catalog matched to the forecast time window and the NZ CSEP testing
region (pyCSEP documentation links in Data and Resources). The primary metrics used in
this manuscript are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item the catalog number test (N-test), which compares $N_{\mathrm{obs}}$
          against the distribution of simulated catalog counts and returns the
          lower-tail quantile $\delta_1$ and upper-tail quantile $\delta_2$;
    \item the catalog magnitude test (M-test), which compares the
          observed magnitude--frequency distribution to the simulated ensemble;
    \item a resampled M-test, which controls for count differences by
          subsampling each simulated catalog to the observed count before
          comparing magnitude distributions;
    \item the marginal log-likelihood (MLL) test;
    \item the catalog spatial test (S-test) and pseudolikelihood test (PL-test),
          interpreted here as support diagnostics given frequent
          \texttt{status=undersampled} reports;
    \item rolling number consistency (rolling KS test $p$-value), which
          assesses whether the temporal allocation of events within the
          7--14~d window is calibrated;
    \item daily empirical CDF-at-observed diagnostics
          ($\hat{F}_d(N_{\mathrm{obs},d})$ for each day bin $d$), a PIT-like
          calibration diagnostic computed from the full ensemble of simulated
          daily counts.
\end{itemize}

Let $\{N_k\}_{k=1}^K$ denote simulated weekly counts ($K=1000$) and
$N_{\mathrm{obs}}$ the observed weekly count. The empirical N-test quantiles
are
\begin{equation}
\delta_1 = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K \mathbf{1}[N_k \le N_{\mathrm{obs}}],
\qquad
\delta_2 = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K \mathbf{1}[N_k \ge N_{\mathrm{obs}}],
\label{eq:n_test}
\end{equation}
with two-tailed consistency threshold $\min(\delta_1,\delta_2)>0.025$.

For magnitude consistency, let $p^{\mathrm{obs}}_j$ and $p^{\mathrm{sim}}_j$
be observed and simulated probabilities in magnitude bin $j$. The pyCSEP
M-test uses the total-variation statistic
\begin{equation}
T_M = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\left|p^{\mathrm{obs}}_j-p^{\mathrm{sim}}_j\right|,
\label{eq:mtest_tv}
\end{equation}
and reports the observed statistic quantile relative to the ensemble. The
resampled M-test applies the same statistic after subsampling each synthetic
catalog to $N_{\mathrm{obs}}$, removing first-order count effects. The MLL
test uses
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MLL} = \sum_j n^{\mathrm{obs}}_j \log p^{\mathrm{sim}}_j,
\label{eq:mll}
\end{equation}
with quantile evaluated against synthetic-catalog MLL values.
These statistics follow catalog-based consistency-test practice
\cite{savran2020}.

For daily bins $d\in\{7\!-\!8,\ldots,13\!-\!14\}$ and simulated counts
$N_{k,d}$, the PIT-like diagnostic is
\begin{equation}
\hat F_d(N_{\mathrm{obs},d})=
\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K \mathbf{1}[N_{k,d}\le N_{\mathrm{obs},d}],
\label{eq:daily_pit}
\end{equation}
and the standardized daily residual is
\begin{equation}
Z_d=\frac{N_{\mathrm{obs},d}-\bar N_d}{s_d},
\qquad
\bar N_d=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K N_{k,d},
\qquad
s_d^2=\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{k=1}^K (N_{k,d}-\bar N_d)^2.
\label{eq:daily_z}
\end{equation}

To quantify full-distribution skill (not only pass/fail consistency), we also
compute proper scoring rules from ensemble samples. For a scalar target $y$
and simulated draws $\{X_k\}_{k=1}^K$, the ensemble CRPS is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CRPS}(F,y)=
\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K |X_k-y|
-\frac{1}{2K^2}\sum_{k=1}^K\sum_{\ell=1}^K |X_k-X_\ell|.
\label{eq:crps}
\end{equation}
For a central $(1-\alpha)$ interval $[L_\alpha,U_\alpha]$, the interval score
is
\begin{equation}
S_\alpha(L_\alpha,U_\alpha;y)=
(U_\alpha-L_\alpha)
+\frac{2}{\alpha}(L_\alpha-y)\mathbf{1}[y<L_\alpha]
+\frac{2}{\alpha}(y-U_\alpha)\mathbf{1}[y>U_\alpha].
\label{eq:interval_score}
\end{equation}
Lower values of both scores indicate better probabilistic forecasts.

To compare full count-distribution shapes across issue times, we compute
Jensen--Shannon divergence (JSD) against the 7~d reference distribution:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{JSD}(P\|Q)=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{KL}(P\|M)+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{KL}(Q\|M),
\quad M=\frac{1}{2}(P+Q).
\label{eq:jsd}
\end{equation}
JSD is bounded on $[0,1]$ bits for base-2 logs, with 0 meaning identical
distributions.

\subsection{Interpretation framework and operational criteria}

To reduce post-hoc interpretation, we use a fixed decision framework throughout
the manuscript. For weekly count consistency, a run is classified as
\emph{underpredict}, \emph{overpredict}, or \emph{pass} from the pyCSEP N-test
tail quantiles:
\[
\text{underpredict if }\delta_2 \ge 0.975,\qquad
\text{overpredict if }\delta_1 \ge 0.975,\qquad
\text{pass otherwise}.
\]
Distribution-level reliability is then assessed with three complementary checks:
(i) rolling KS calibration ($p>0.05$ interpreted as non-extreme),
(ii) empirical 90\% interval coverage of $N_{\mathrm{obs}}$ (targeting
coverage near nominal), and (iii) proper-score comparison (weekly CRPS and
interval score). For benchmark handover decisions, ETAS and RJ are compared by
weekly CRPS in the same fixed window.

In the operational decision table (Section~\ref{sec:threshold_sens}),
\textbf{GO} is assigned only if all four conditions hold:
N-test pass, non-extreme rolling calibration, non-zero 90\% coverage, and
ETAS weekly CRPS not worse than RJ. Otherwise the run is \textbf{NO-GO}.
This criterion is intentionally conservative and sequence-specific; it is
used here as a transparent issuance screen for the archived Kaik\={o}ura runs.

\subsection{Uncertainty intervals and segmented issue-time analysis}

To report uncertainty on headline metrics (count ratio and proper scores), we
use nonparametric bootstrap resampling of the 1000-catalog ensembles with
replacement. For a scalar statistic $T(\mathbf{X})$ computed from an ensemble
$\mathbf{X}=(X_1,\ldots,X_K)$, bootstrap replicates are
\begin{equation}
T^{*(b)} = T\!\left(X_{I^{(b)}_1},\ldots,X_{I^{(b)}_K}\right), \qquad
I^{(b)}_j \sim \mathrm{Unif}\{1,\ldots,K\},
\label{eq:bootstrap}
\end{equation}
and we report percentile 95\% intervals
$[\hat q_{0.025}(T^*), \hat q_{0.975}(T^*)]$.
For daily diagnostics, resampling is done at catalog level (rows of the
daily-count matrix), preserving within-catalog day-to-day dependence.

To quantify issue-time regime transitions, we fit segmented mean models over the
ordered issue-time index with 0, 1, or 2 breakpoints. For response values
$y_i$, breakpoint set $B$, and segment means $\mu_s$, the objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SSE}(B)=\sum_{s}\sum_{i\in s(B)} (y_i-\mu_s)^2.
\label{eq:sse_segment}
\end{equation}
We evaluate count-ratio and $\log_{10}$ weekly-CRPS series using exhaustive
search with minimum segment length of two issue-time points. Model comparison is
reported via BIC and permutation tests (10,000 permutations) for the
improvement of segmented models relative to simpler baselines. Breakpoint
stability is summarized with bootstrap support (5,000 replicates) from
run-specific metric bootstrap distributions.

Because spatial and pseudolikelihood tests are frequently
\texttt{status=undersampled}, we report support-failure counts explicitly.
Let $R_0=\{\text{cells with forecast rate }0\}$. Then
\begin{equation}
n^{(S)}_0 = \sum_{c\in R_0} N_{\mathrm{obs}}(c),
\label{eq:zero_spatial}
\end{equation}
and in space--magnitude bins $b$ with zero forecast rate $\Lambda_b$,
\begin{equation}
n^{(SM)}_0 = \sum_{b:\Lambda_b=0} N_{\mathrm{obs}}(b).
\label{eq:zero_spacemag}
\end{equation}
These are interpreted as primary support diagnostics.

These distribution-based diagnostics are motivated by prior ETAS forecast
distribution work showing that mean behavior alone is insufficient and that
full predictive distributions can be strongly overdispersed
\cite{harte2017dist,helmstetter2003,zhuang2013critical,savran2020}.

\subsection{Paired benchmark comparison tests}

To compare ETAS skill against a benchmark forecast on the same space--time--
magnitude grid, we use pyCSEP paired T- and W-tests based on event-wise
information gain per observed event \cite{savran2020}. Let
$\Lambda_A=\sum_b \lambda_A(b)$ and $\Lambda_B=\sum_b \lambda_B(b)$ be total
forecast rates over bins $b$, and let $b_i$ be the bin of observed event $i$.
For bins with nonzero support in both forecasts, per-event information gain is
\begin{equation}
IG_i = \log \lambda_A(b_i) - \log \lambda_B(b_i),
\label{eq:ig_event}
\end{equation}
and the normalized mean information gain is
\begin{equation}
\overline{IG}=
\frac{1}{N^*}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N^*} IG_i - (\Lambda_A-\Lambda_B)\right),
\label{eq:ig_mean}
\end{equation}
where $N^*$ is the number of observed events retained after support filtering.
When events fall in zero-rate bins for either forecast, pyCSEP reports an
undersampled condition and recomputes on the retained set.

The paired T-test statistic is
\begin{equation}
T=\frac{\overline{IG}}{s_{IG}/\sqrt{N^*}},
\qquad
s_{IG}^2=\frac{1}{N^*-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N^*}(IG_i-\overline{IG})^2,
\label{eq:paired_t}
\end{equation}
with two-sided $p$-value from the Student-$t$ distribution. The paired W-test
applies the Wilcoxon signed-rank statistic to $\{IG_i\}$ as a nonparametric
robustness check against non-Gaussian tails. In this manuscript, we interpret
both tests jointly: T-test for mean information-gain effect size and W-test for
sign consistency under weaker distributional assumptions.

\subsection{Reasenberg--Jones reference benchmark and threshold sensitivity}
\label{sec:method_rj}

To provide a low-dimensional operational baseline, we constructed a
Reasenberg--Jones (RJ) count benchmark with fixed temporal parameters
$p=1.0$ and $c=0.1$~day and issue-time-specific productivity estimated from the
observed training count. For issue time $T_i$, the RJ intensity is
\begin{equation}
\lambda_{RJ}(t\mid T_i)=K_i(t+c)^{-p}, \qquad t>0,
\label{eq:rj_intensity}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
\hat K_i=\frac{N_{[0,T_i)}}{\int_0^{T_i}(t+c)^{-p}\,dt},
\quad
\hat N^{RJ}_{[7,14]}(T_i)=
\hat K_i\int_7^{14}(t+c)^{-p}\,dt.
\label{eq:rj_mean}
\end{equation}
We evaluate RJ as a Poisson predictive distribution with mean
$\hat N^{RJ}_{[7,14]}(T_i)$, using the same observed target
$N_{\mathrm{obs}}=323$ as ETAS. This benchmark is intentionally simple:
it isolates whether ETAS issue-time behavior improves on a robust count-only
reference.

For reproducibility of the RJ training counts, we use a stitched observed
catalog:
\texttt{build/pycsep/cache/2016p858000\_d0\_14p5\_mc3\_observed.csv}
for the first 2~h and
\texttt{build/pycsep\_2h/cache/2016p858000\_d0p08333\_14p5\_mc3\_observed.csv}
for 2~h--14.5~d, maintaining consistency with the archived fixed-horizon
7--14~d target catalog.

We also compute magnitude-threshold sensitivity from the same fixed-horizon
simulation ensembles (no rerun), evaluating count consistency and CRPS for
$M\geq3,4,5,6$ in the 7--14~d window.

The generic run sits near the NZ prior mean. The 2~h--1~d fits converge to a
\emph{low-cascade local minimum}: $a$ is strongly negative ($-3.05$ to
$-3.20$), $c$ is very small (0.0006--0.0021~d), and the indirect triggering
fraction is only 2.3--3.9\%, compared to 32.6\% in the converged 7~d fit.
This is not a gradual evolution but a discrete MLE failure mode: with only
hours of data, the likelihood surface is dominated by the mainshock's direct
contribution, and the MLE locks into a solution where cascading is
near-zero.

A \textbf{grid resolution artifact} is apparent at 12~h and 1~d, which produce
identical fitted parameters ($a_{ms}=-1.75$, $a=-3.05$, $p=0.90$,
$c=0.0006$~d) despite the additional 12 hours of data. This is consistent
with the finite resolution of the $21\times21\times16\times16$ MLE grid:
the two data windows are insufficiently different to move the grid optimum,
confirming that any apparent distinction between the 12~h and 1~d forecasts
is not parameter-driven.

The transition between 1~d and 2~d marks a qualitative regime shift. At 2~d the
MLE \emph{escapes} the low-cascade minimum: $a$ rises to $-2.00$, $c$ jumps to
0.1~d, and indirect triggering reaches 30.6\%---comparable to the 7~d value.
This \textbf{branching barrier} (crossed between 1~d and 2~d) represents the
minimum data window needed for the MLE to identify that aftershock-to-aftershock
cascading is a significant component of sequence productivity.

However, 2~d and 3~d data are still insufficient to identify the Omori decay
exponent accurately. Both fits return $p=0.90$, while the 7~d fit gives
$p=1.10$. Since $p$ controls the long-term tail of the Omori law, a fit with
$p=0.90$ generates too many events at days 7--14 relative to the 7~d
reference-run decay rate. This \textbf{Omori barrier} is only fully crossed
with 7~d of data.

These two barriers are conceptually distinct and require different amounts of
information. The branching barrier depends primarily on observing enough
aftershock-to-aftershock pairs to lift the $a$-value estimate; the Omori
barrier depends on observing the temporal curvature of the decay rate over a
sufficiently long baseline. Separating these two constraints is useful for
targeting model improvements by issue-time regime.

are now correctly identified. The PL-test and S-test remain undersampled (see
Section~\ref{sec:spatial}), but the count and temporal calibration are sound.

\subsection{Magnitude--frequency consistency across issue times}
\label{sec:mag_freq}

An important result of the fixed-horizon experiment is that magnitude--frequency
skill is \emph{decoupled} from count skill: the Gutenberg--Richter distribution
is well-captured regardless of issue time.

The resampled M-test passes for all eight runs, with lower-tail quantiles of
0.744--0.852 (Table~\ref{tab:counts}). The resampled test controls for count
differences by drawing a subsample of each simulated catalog matched to the
observed count before computing the total variation distance. Its universal
passage means that the relative frequency of magnitudes among simulated events
matches the observed magnitude distribution for all issue times.

The MLL test similarly passes for all eight runs, with lower-tail quantiles of
0.324--0.390, confirming that the log-likelihood of the observed magnitudes
under the simulated ensemble is well within the central body of the simulated
distribution.

The simple (unsampled) M-test fails for the early windows (lower-tail quantile
$\approx1.000$ for 2~h--1~d). This is a count artifact: with simulation
medians of 45--51, the simulated magnitude probability mass function is
necessarily sparse and concentrated at lower magnitudes relative to the full
distribution, producing large total variation distance when compared to 323
observed events. The resampled M-test correctly identifies this as a count
confound rather than a genuine GR failure.

The operational implication is direct: the $b=1.0$ assumption underlying the NZ
ETAS implementation is robust across all issue times for this sequence. Any
operational upgrade effort should therefore prioritize count calibration
(the branching and Omori barriers described above) rather than magnitude
recalibration.

\subsection{Daily decomposition and error compensation at 7~d}
\label{sec:daily_comp}

Using the full 1000-catalog ensembles, we decomposed the fixed-horizon
forecast into seven daily bins (7--8, \ldots, 13--14~d) and computed a PIT-like
diagnostic $\hat{F}_d(N_{\mathrm{obs},d})=\Pr(N_{\mathrm{sim},d}\leq
N_{\mathrm{obs},d})$ per day and issue time
(Figure~\ref{fig:daily_decomposition}).

This reveals a stronger and more specific regime structure than weekly totals
alone:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Regime~1 (generic and 2~h--1~d) is one-sided underprediction
    in every daily bin.} For the 2~h--1~d branch, all 28 daily bins have
    $\hat{F}_d \ge 0.993$ (mean 0.998), and standardized residuals are strongly
    positive throughout (typical $Z>+3$, often $>+7$). There are no daily sign
    flips in residuals.

    \item \textbf{Regime~2 (2~d--3~d) is one-sided overprediction in every daily
    bin.} Across these 14 daily bins, $\hat{F}_d$ lies in [0.001, 0.144]
    (mean 0.038), with 10/14 bins at $\hat{F}_d \le 0.05$. Residual signs are
    uniformly positive in forecast-minus-observed space (no sign flips).

    \item \textbf{Regime~3 (7~d) is mixed-sign daily behavior with compensation.}
    The 7~d run has $\hat{F}_d$ spanning 0.033--0.801 and three daily sign
    flips (Table~\ref{tab:daily7d}). Its weekly total is near-calibrated because
    overprediction in some bins (e.g., days 7--8 and 10--11, where $\hat{F}_d=0.193$
    and 0.033 respectively) is compensated by underprediction in others
    (e.g., days 8--9 and 13--14, where $\hat{F}_d=0.768$ and 0.801), not because
    each day is individually well-calibrated.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Probabilistic score diagnostics}
\label{sec:prob_scores}

We computed proper-score and distribution-distance diagnostics from the full
ensemble outputs (\texttt{publication\_probabilistic\_scores.csv}; Figure
\ref{fig:prob_scores}). These metrics sharpen the regime interpretation by
quantifying not just directional bias but full predictive-distribution quality.

At weekly scale, the 7~d run is clearly superior:
CRPS = 14.48 and 90\% interval score = 204.15, compared with
CRPS = 144.66--150.57 and interval score = 2090--2128 for the 2~d--3~d runs,
and CRPS = 256--266 with interval score = 4774--4911 for the 2~h--1~d runs.
Thus the 7~d weekly predictive distribution is improved by about one order of
magnitude relative to both early and intermediate issue times.

At daily scale, the same ordering persists:
daily-mean CRPS = 5.74 at 7~d, versus 20.63--22.33 at 2~d--3~d and
29.87--31.17 at 2~h--1~d. Daily 90\% interval-score penalties are also much
lower at 7~d (52.75) than at 2~d--3~d (235) and 2~h--1~d (514--545).
Calibration diagnostics are consistent with the regime picture:
mean $|\mathrm{PIT}-0.5|$ is 0.230 at 7~d, 0.462 at 2~d--3~d, and
0.498--0.499 at 2~h--1~d; the fraction of extreme daily PIT bins
($<0.05$ or $>0.95$) is 0.143, 0.714, and 1.0, respectively.

Distributional distance to the 7~d reference is similarly diagnostic.
Weekly JSD is 0.728 for 2~d--3~d and 1.0 for 2~h--1~d; daily-mean JSD is
0.470 for 2~d--3~d and $\sim$0.98 for 2~h--1~d.
This confirms that the 2~d--3~d forecasts are \emph{closer in shape} to the
calibrated regime but still substantially miscalibrated, whereas the 2~h--1~d
forecasts are distributionally almost disjoint from the 7~d behavior.

The spatial support pattern follows the fitted kernel width: runs with fewer

unsupported observations (10--22) because more observed events fall within the

observed events in zero-rate locations, suggesting persistent limitation from
the isotropic kernel assumption rather than from data volume alone.

sequence-specific runs (2374--2653), despite using more data. This reflects
how the broader late-window kernel redistributes mass across a wider halo while
still leaving distant cells empty under the discrete grid. The operationally
relevant quantity is therefore not the number of zero-rate cells alone, but the
number of observed events in those cells ($n^{(S)}_0$), which remains non-zero

in these runs and events are allocated incorrectly within the 7--14~d window.
The 2~h--1~d runs fail strongly ($p$ as low as $2\times10^{-21}$)
because the entire simulation count is compressed toward the beginning of the
window by near-zero $c$ values.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Three failure regimes}

The three-regime structure identified in this analysis is only observable under
the fixed-horizon design. In any experiment where verification windows vary with
issue time, count differences reflect a mixture of issue-time effects and
horizon effects that cannot be cleanly disentangled. The uniform verification
target ($N_{\mathrm{obs}}=323$ in all cases) reveals that the transition from
underprediction to overprediction between 1~d and 2~d is a genuine property of
the MLE landscape, not an artifact of different observation windows.

Mechanistically, Regime~1 (generic and 2~h--1~d) is a mainshock-dominated fit
with insufficient information to recover cascading; Regime~2 (2~d--3~d)
recovers branching but not Omori decay ($p=0.90$); and Regime~3 (7~d) recovers
both branching and decay and is calibrated at the weekly level.

\subsection{\texttt{timeDependentMc} is necessary but not sufficient}

The \texttt{timeDependentMc} correction addresses catalog incompleteness during
fitting by down-weighting events in incomplete intervals. This is a necessary
ingredient for early ETAS forecasting, consistent with the literature
\cite{omi2013,omi2014,page2016,seif2017,hainzl2024}. However, the results here
show clearly that it is not sufficient to recover forecast skill for issue times
of 1~d or less. The 2~h--1~d runs all have \texttt{timeDependentMc=true}, yet
they all produce severely underpredictive forecasts with N-test failures at
$\delta_2=1.000$.

The key point is that \texttt{timeDependentMc} controls \emph{which} events are
used in the likelihood, but does not guarantee that enough information exists
to identify branching. Completeness correction and identifiability are separate
requirements; the first-day runs in this archive satisfy the former but not the
latter.

\subsection{Two identifiability barriers as a conceptual framework}

The sequence of regime transitions implies two distinct identifiability
barriers, each requiring a different amount of data:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The branching barrier} (crossed between 1~d and 2~d): the
    MLE requires enough aftershock-to-aftershock pairs to identify the $a$-value
    and distinguish a genuinely cascading sequence from a mainshock-dominated
    sequence. Until this barrier is crossed, the indirect triggering fraction is
    near zero and count forecasts are severely underpredictive.

    \item \textbf{The Omori barrier} (crossed between 3~d and 7~d): the MLE
    requires a sufficiently long temporal baseline to resolve the curvature of
    the Omori decay law and estimate $p$ accurately. Until this barrier is
    crossed, $p$ is underestimated and the forecast rate at days 7--14 is
    systematically too high.
\end{enumerate}

This framework suggests two targeted improvements, each addressing one barrier.
The branching barrier might be lowered by using stronger Bayesian priors on the
$a$-value or by incorporating external sequence-similarity information during
the first day. The Omori barrier is more fundamental because $p$ estimation
requires observing the temporal slope over an extended window, but it could be
addressed by a tight Bayesian prior on $p$ derived from regional or global
aftershock statistics.

\subsection{How segmented tests inform the barrier interpretation}

The segmented issue-time tests in Section~\ref{sec:segmented_tests} provide a
formal complement to the regime interpretation. For count ratio, the best
one-break model places the transition between 1~d and 2~d
(permutation $p=0.036$), directly supporting the branching-barrier location.
For $\log_{10}$ weekly CRPS, one- and two-break models are suggestive but not
significant at $\alpha=0.05$, which is expected with only eight issue-time
points and one sequence. We therefore treat the late-window transition as
inference from convergent evidence (parameter shift, rolling KS, PIT/coverage,
and score collapse at 7~d), with segmented tests used as supportive rather than
standalone evidence.

\subsection{Double failure: why bias alone understates the operational problem}
\label{sec:double_failure}

The interval diagnostics in Section~\ref{sec:prob_scores} show that
early-window and intermediate-window failures are not only directional-bias
problems. In every non-calibrated run, the 90\% predictive interval excludes
the observed weekly count:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Regime~1 (2~h--1~d)}: the 90\% interval spans 50--84 events,
    while the observed count is 323. This indicates strong underprediction
    combined with severe under-coverage.

    \item \textbf{Regime~2 (2~d--3~d)}: the 90\% interval spans 411--742 events,
    so the observed count 323 lies below the lower bound. This indicates
    overprediction combined with severe under-coverage.
\end{itemize}

This pattern has direct implications for validation practice. Reporting only
point summaries (mean or median) can hide whether predictive uncertainty is
reliable. Operational reports should therefore include interval diagnostics and
historical coverage performance alongside count medians.

\subsection{Operational handover criterion from RJ and reliability diagnostics}

The RJ comparison and operational decision table (Section~\ref{sec:rj_results},
Table~\ref{tab:operational_decision}) suggest a practical handover rule for this
sequence class. For issue times up to 3~d, ETAS either underpredicts or
overpredicts with zero 90\% coverage, and RJ has lower weekly CRPS in all seven
non-7d cases. Only at 7~d does ETAS simultaneously pass count consistency,
temporal calibration, and interval coverage, while also outperforming RJ in CRPS.

This implies that an operational ETAS product should be gated by \emph{joint}
criteria, not by issue time alone: count consistency (N-test), temporal
allocation (rolling KS / PIT behavior), uncertainty reliability (coverage), and
benchmark dominance (ETAS score better than RJ). In this archive, the gate opens
only for the 7~d run.

\subsection{Implications for prospective deployment}

The present archive is retrospective and sequence-specific, but the decision
logic can be implemented prospectively with no methodological change. A practical
workflow is: issue early RJ and ETAS in parallel, evaluate ETAS against fixed
reliability gates (count consistency, temporal calibration, interval coverage),
and promote ETAS to primary only once the gate is satisfied for the active
sequence class. This preserves rapid initial issuance while reducing the chance
that an under-resolved ETAS fit is treated as a fully calibrated forecast.

\subsection{Magnitude-threshold robustness and where statistical power collapses}

Threshold sensitivity confirms that the regime structure is robust at
moderate magnitudes: the same underpredict/overpredict/calibrated pattern
persists for $M\geq4$ as for $M\geq3$.
At higher thresholds ($M\geq5$ and especially $M\geq6$), observed counts in the
fixed horizon are too small for strong consistency discrimination. This is a
power limitation, not evidence that high-magnitude tail behavior is fully
resolved. Consequently, issue-time conclusions in this manuscript should be
interpreted as strongest for the moderate-magnitude operational count range.

\subsection{Expected-count consistency and reporting unresolved $E[N]$}

The divergence between $E[N]$ and simulation medians in the early-window runs
is a practically important finding. For the 12~h and 1~d parameter sets, the
analytical expectation exceeds both empirical medians and empirical means by
about one order of magnitude. The synthesis diagnostics (Section~\ref{sec:diagfig})
show this is not a median-only artifact, so it should be treated as an
analytical-versus-stochastic inconsistency requiring dedicated QC.

In operational practice, the mean $E[N]$ is the quantity most naturally
associated with the first-moment prediction of a point process, and it may be
the quantity displayed to emergency managers or communicated to the public. For
better-behaved runs (2~d--7~d), $E[N]$ and empirical summaries are broadly
consistent and either may be acceptable. But for early issue times in this
sequence, reporting unresolved analytical $E[N]$ values would overstate the
forecast by large factors. Operational protocols should require empirical
ensemble summaries (median and central intervals) whenever analytical
consistency checks fail.

\subsection{Bayesian priors on $p$ for Regime~2}

The Regime~2 overprediction (2~d--3~d) arises specifically from $p$
underestimation. Once the branching barrier is crossed, the cascade structure
is essentially correct, and the discrepancy is concentrated in the Omori
exponent. This suggests a targeted intervention: a Bayesian prior on $p$
derived from regional or global aftershock statistics would constrain the MLE
away from the shallow-decay solution at early times without requiring additional
data.

For a sequence like Kaik\={o}ura, where regional Omori statistics might favor
$p\approx1.0$--$1.1$, a moderately informative prior could pull the 2~d and
3~d fits toward the correct $p=1.10$ value, potentially moving those runs into
or near the calibrated regime. This is a structurally different improvement
from \texttt{timeDependentMc}: it targets a different stage of the MLE problem
(parameter bounding rather than data selection) and a different failure mode
(Regime~2 rather than Regime~1).

\subsection{Relation to the literature}

These results are consistent with the early-forecast ETAS literature but add
specificity that previous studies could not provide. Omi et al.\ showed that
very early ETAS forecasting can work only when incompleteness is handled
explicitly \cite{omi2013,omi2014,omi2015}. Page et al.\ identified
time-dependent catalog incompleteness as an essential operational ingredient
\cite{page2016}, and Seif et al.\ quantified how strongly missing early events
can distort ETAS parameters \cite{seif2017}. The Kaik\={o}ura case study of
Harte likewise found that the earliest real-time ETAS forecasts were compromised
by both early catalog limitations and evolving event metadata \cite{harte2019}.

The archived NZ results fit that pattern but add three specific new observations.
First, the branching/Omori barrier distinction is more cleanly visible here than
in previous analyses because of the fixed-horizon design. Second, the explicit
analytical-versus-stochastic expected-count inconsistency check provides a
practical QC criterion for early operational ETAS products. Third, the daily
decomposition (Section~\ref{sec:daily_comp}) shows that weekly calibration can
arise from compensation of opposite-sign daily errors, a point aligned with
distribution-focused ETAS forecast studies \cite{harte2017dist,savran2020}.

The persistent spatial support limitation (16--25 observed events in zero-rate
cells across all runs) is consistent with the broader observation in aftershock
forecasting reviews \cite{hardebeck2024} that operational value depends not only
on count calibration but on spatial accuracy. Isotropic spatial kernels are a
known limitation of the basic ETAS formulation; the fact that this limitation
persists even in the well-calibrated 7~d run suggests that kernel improvements
would be complementary to, and separate from, the temporal calibration
improvements identified here.

\section{Limitations}

Five limitations should be kept explicit when interpreting these results.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Single-sequence scope}: this manuscript analyzes one
    operationally important sequence (Kaik\={o}ura). Regime structure and
    breakpoint locations should be tested prospectively across additional NZ and
    global sequences before broad generalization.

    \item \textbf{Finite issue-time sample for segmentation}: formal segmented
    tests are based on eight issue-time points. This provides useful structure
    tests but limited statistical power, especially for detecting secondary
    breakpoints.

    \item \textbf{Finite-horizon branching diagnostic}: $\bar n_H$ is defined on
    a fixed horizon ($H=7$~d) to remain well-defined for $p\le1$. It is a
    horizon-specific stability indicator, not an asymptotic branching ratio.

    \item \textbf{Analytical-versus-stochastic expected-count mismatch}: early
    runs show unresolved divergence between summary-file $E[N]$ and empirical
    simulation distributions. Operational recommendations in this manuscript
    therefore emphasize empirical ensemble summaries (median and intervals) for
    early windows.

    \item \textbf{Model-structure and parameterization constraints}: the tested
    implementation uses an isotropic spatial triggering kernel, fixed
    magnitude-distribution slope ($b=1$), and finite-grid optimization for
    $(a_{ms},a,p,c)$. Different kernel forms, priors, or optimization schemes
    could shift breakpoint locations or reduce support failures.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusions}

The fixed-horizon issue-time experiment for the 2016 $M_w$ 7.8 Kaik\={o}ura
sequence produces the following principal conclusions.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The fixed-horizon design (all runs verifying days 7--14,
    $N_{\mathrm{obs}}=323$) resolves three issue-time regimes that are obscured
    in variable-horizon comparisons.

    \item \textbf{Regime~1} (generic and 2~h--1~d) remains strongly
    underpredictive (medians 45--86; ratios 0.14--0.27) and converges to a
    low-cascade solution (indirect triggering 2--9\%). This indicates that
    \texttt{timeDependentMc} alone does not overcome early branching
    identifiability limits.

    \item \textbf{Regime~2} (2~d--3~d) recovers branching but not Omori decay:
    fitted $p=0.90$ versus 1.10 in the 7~d reference fit, yielding systematic
    overprediction (medians 492--499; ratios 1.52--1.55).

    \item \textbf{Regime~3} (7~d) is the only calibrated run in this archive
    (median 334; ratio 1.03; N-test $(\delta_1,\delta_2)=(0.594,0.411)$;
    rolling KS $p=0.98$), with daily diagnostics indicating mixed-sign residual
    compensation within the week.

    \item Segmented and bootstrap analyses support a primary transition near
    1--2~d (count-ratio one-break permutation $p=0.036$), with weaker evidence
    for a second late-window breakpoint in this single-sequence,
    eight-issue-time dataset.

    \item Probabilistic diagnostics show a consistent reliability ordering:
    7~d minimizes weekly CRPS (14.48) and is the only run with 90\% interval
    coverage of the observed weekly count (100\%); all non-7~d runs have 0\%
    coverage.

    \item Against a fixed-parameter RJ baseline ($p=1.0$, $c=0.1$~d), ETAS is
    superior only at 7~d (weekly CRPS 14.48 vs 19.99). For earlier issue times,
    RJ is less miscalibrated, supporting an evidence-based model handover rule.

    \item Magnitude behavior is stable across issue times (resampled M-test and
    MLL pass throughout), and the same regime structure persists at $M\geq4$.
    At $M\geq5$ and above, low observed counts limit inferential power.

    \item Spatial-support limitations remain in all runs (16--25 observed events
    in zero-rate cells). Together with the early expected-count mismatch
    (summary-file $E[N]$ versus empirical ensembles), this supports two practical
    recommendations: use empirical ensemble summaries for early windows, and
    prioritize model development on (i) branching/Omori identifiability and
    (ii) spatial-kernel support.
\end{enumerate}
